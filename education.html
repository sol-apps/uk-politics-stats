Twenty years of PISA, four nations

England has just posted its best science score since 2012. Wales has just posted its worst result in every subject since it started taking the test. The two things happened under the same economy, three years apart, and the gap between them is now wider than at any point in the series.

The question this data is usually asked to settle

Wales is poorer than England, so it is tempting to read the Welsh result as a poverty result — the same schools doing the same job with a harder intake. The PISA data can test that directly, because every pupil is scored on the OECD's index of economic, social and cultural status (ESCS) and the results are published by quartile of that index.

If Wales's problem were the composition of its intake — more poor children, same schooling — then Welsh pupils at any given point on the socio-economic scale would score about the same as English pupils at that point, and only the national average would differ. That is a falsifiable claim, and the data falsifies it.

A composition effect cannot produce a gap that widens as you move up the income scale. Whatever separates England from Wales is doing most of its work among the pupils least likely to be held back by poverty.

Wales has the narrowest gap in the UK. That is not the good news it sounds like

The distance between a nation's most and least advantaged pupils is the standard measure of how much economic background determines results. On that measure Wales does well — better than England, better than the OECD.

When did the four nations actually diverge?

Twice, in opposite directions, and neither time in 2012.

Education has never been a single UK system. Scotland's has been separate since before the Union — the Acts of Union in 1707 preserved distinct Scottish institutions, and it has had its own qualifications, curriculum and inspectorate ever since. Northern Ireland has had its own Ministry of Education since June 1921, at partition, and kept academic selection at eleven when England moved to comprehensives. Devolution in 1999 changed who decided policy in those two nations, not whether they were separate.

Wales was the exception, and it left first. Wales was administratively joined to England: the Education Reform Act 1988 created a National Curriculum for “England and Wales” as one jurisdiction. Devolution in 1999 gave Wales the power to diverge and it used it quickly — school performance tables dropped in 2001, national curriculum tests at Key Stages 1 to 3 ended under Rhodri Morgan's “clear red water” from 2001–02, the Foundation Phase from 2004, the Welsh Baccalaureate from 2003, and no academies or free schools at any point.

Then England left, harder, a decade later. The 2010 Coalition wave was not a tweak: the Academies Act 2010 and mass academisation, free schools from 2011, the EBacc measure in 2010, the phonics screening check made statutory in 2012, a new National Curriculum in 2014, reformed linear GCSEs from 2015, and Progress 8 replacing the old five-A*-to-C measure in 2016. Where Wales had removed accountability machinery, England added a great deal of it. These are two separate divergences roughly a decade apart, and the second one is the bigger break.

Which shows up in the data?

Absolute scores cannot answer this, because the whole OECD fell over the same period and every nation fell with it. Measuring each nation against the OECD trend average strips that out.

That is the strongest timing evidence on this page for the 2010 English reforms, and it points where you would expect: the first cohorts substantially schooled under them start sitting PISA from 2015, and England's advantage grows in every cycle from there. But three things stop it being proof.

England's gain is resistance, not improvement. Its 2025 scores in maths (492) and reading (497) are below its 2012 scores. The advantage grew because the OECD fell 26 and 31 points and England fell three. That is a real achievement and it is not the same claim as “the reforms raised attainment”.

The one point that most looks like a payoff is the least trustworthy. England's absolute peak in both maths (504) and reading (505) is 2018 — precisely the cohort that went through secondary school from 2014, and precisely the round in which Jerrim found England's sample skewed high enough to inflate maths by about 11 points. The apparent reform dividend and the documented sampling problem are the same size, in the same year, and cannot be told apart.

The subject that jumped is the wrong one. England's one large absolute rise is science, up 13 points in 2025. Reading and maths were flat. If phonics and the curriculum reforms were driving the improvement, reading is where it should show first — and reading did not move.

So: England's policy did change sharply around 2010, and England has clearly outperformed the international trend since. Whether the first caused the second is not something seven three-yearly observations of four jurisdictions can establish, and the strongest-looking evidence for it has a known measurement problem sitting on top of it.

What this data does establish is narrower and firmer: the England–Wales gap is not explained by how poor Wales is — it survives holding socio-economic status constant, and it is largest exactly where poverty is least relevant. The money page tests that against GDP per head and child poverty directly.

The gap itself

Scotland remains a separate puzzle rather than a divergence story, since it never shared a system to diverge from. It is the only UK nation to have lost ground against the OECD since 2012, and its 39-point fall in maths since 2006 is the steepest decline in the UK. Curriculum for Excellence, phased into all schools from August 2010, is the usual suspect, and the timing fits in the loose sense that the first fully-affected cohorts sat PISA from 2015. It is also vigorously contested, including by the OECD's own 2021 review, and this site has nothing to add to that argument.

What to distrust in these numbers

The UK's exclusion rate is above the OECD's limit and rising. 7.6% of sampled schools were excluded in 2025, against a 5% target — up from 4.9% in 2022 and 5.4% in 2018. The OECD accepted the results for trend comparison; the direction of that drift still flatters the UK, because excluded schools are not a random sample.

England's 2025 participation met the standard; 2022's did not, across much of the UK. England reached a 90% weighted school response rate in 2025 (target 85%) after replacement schools were brought in, and 83% of pupils (target 80%). The 2022 round is flagged in the official reports as failing one or more sampling standards, which matters because 2022 is the comparison point for England's 13-point science rise.

Independent work has found England's PISA sample skews high. Analysis of the 2018 data by John Jerrim found England's maths mean was roughly 11 points above what a properly representative sample would have given, because participating pupils had higher prior attainment and lower free school meal eligibility than the population.

2025's cohort sat primary school through the pandemic. These pupils were 10 or 11 when schools closed. That is true in every country, which is what makes the international comparison still worth something, but it is not true to the same degree everywhere.

None of this is a reason to ignore PISA. It is a reason to trust the within-country comparisons — England against Wales, quartile against quartile, both measured the same way in the same round — more than the league-table position. That is what this site does with it.
