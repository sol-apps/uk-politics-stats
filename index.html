Charts on UK public policy

Every number here comes from a primary statistical source — the OECD, the Office for National Statistics, the Department for Education, the devolved governments — and every chart says which one. Where the data cannot answer a question, the page says so instead of guessing.

  • Education: PISA across the four nations

    Every PISA result for England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland from 2006 to the 2025 round published on 8 September 2026. England's science score jumped 13 points; Wales fell further behind in all three subjects.

  • Money & results: does economics explain the gaps?

    The question this site was built to answer. Tested against GDP per head, child poverty and the socio-economic attainment gradient. Short answer: no — and the reason why is more interesting than the correlation would have been.

  • England by region

    PISA cannot be split into English regions — its sample is not built for it. England's own GCSE data can, for every pupil in every state school. It shows something PISA cannot: the region with the most disadvantaged pupils gets the best results.

How this site handles data

Three rules, applied throughout.

Primary sources only. Figures are read out of the published data tables, not out of news coverage of them. Where two sources disagree, the page says which one it used and why.

Caveats sit with the chart, not in a footnote nobody reads. PISA is a sample survey with real sampling problems in the UK; GCSE grades in 2020 and 2021 were teacher-assessed and are not comparable with exam years. Both facts are on the charts they affect.

Correlations are reported honestly, including when they are weak or when they vanish under a second look. With four nations you can find a correlation of 0.93 with almost anything. The money page shows one, then shows why it means nothing.
