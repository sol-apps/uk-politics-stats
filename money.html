Does money explain the gaps?

This page exists because the correlation looked convincing and turned out not to be. With four nations you can find a correlation of 0.93 with almost anything. What follows is the test, then the second test that breaks it.

The correlation that looks convincing

Rank the four nations by GDP per head and you get England, Scotland, Northern Ireland, Wales. Rank them by PISA score and you get England, Northern Ireland, Scotland, Wales. One swap. The correlation is +0.93.

The test that breaks it

A cross-sectional correlation between rich places and good schools tells you nothing about whether money causes results, because rich places differ from poor places in a hundred other ways. The usable test is change: when a nation gets richer, do its results improve?

Between 2012 and 2023 all four nations got materially richer per head — between 10% and 14% in real terms. Over the same period three of the four fell in PISA and the fourth was flat.

Pool all four nations and all seven PISA cycles — twenty-eight observations rather than four — and the correlation between GDP per head and PISA score falls to +0.48, an R² of 0.23. The strong relationship was an artefact of having only four points.

Poverty

GDP per head is a poor measure of whether children are poor. It counts output where it is produced, not income where people live, which is why London's figure is inflated by commuters. The direct measure is the share of children in relative low income after housing costs.

The arithmetic is worth doing explicitly, because it is the whole argument. England's child poverty rate is 29%; Wales's is 32%. If poverty were the mechanism, moving three per cent of Welsh children across the poverty line would have to be worth 38 PISA points. It is not close. Within England, the entire distance from the most disadvantaged quarter of pupils to the most advantaged — the full sweep of the income distribution — is 81 points in science. A three-point shift in the share below one threshold is worth a couple of points at most.

Inequality

The third candidate is inequality rather than poverty: perhaps Wales's problem is that background matters more there. The opposite is true, and it is measured in the same test.

So what is left

Three economic explanations were available and none survives contact with the data. GDP per head correlates across nations and not over time. Child poverty does not correlate at all. Wales is more equal than England, not less. And, from the education page, the England–Wales gap holds at every point on the socio-economic distribution and is largest among the most advantaged pupils — the ones whose results are least constrained by money.

That is a negative result, and negative results are worth stating plainly: the England–Wales gap in PISA is not an economic phenomenon. Whatever is producing it is acting on pupils whose family circumstances are already comfortable, which points at what happens in schools rather than at what happens to household budgets.

This site is not going to take the next step and name the policy. Seven three-yearly observations of four jurisdictions cannot separate a curriculum change from an accountability change from a teacher-supply change, and any chart that claims otherwise is doing rhetoric. What the data supports is the elimination, not the identification. The regional page pushes on the same question from the other side — nine regions under one education policy, with very different economies.
