England by region

Nine regions, one education policy, very different economies. That makes England the cleanest natural experiment in the UK for the question the rest of this site is asking — and the answer it gives is not the one the national comparison suggests.

First, why this page is not about PISA

The obvious thing to do would be to split England's PISA score by region. It cannot be done honestly. PISA's England sample is roughly 4,700 pupils in 165 schools, drawn to produce one national estimate. Cut that nine ways and the confidence intervals swallow the differences. The Department for Education produced experimental regional estimates once, from the 2015 round, with intervals wide enough that most regions were statistically indistinguishable, and has not repeated the exercise.

England's own data has the opposite problem, which is to say no problem: Attainment 8 covers every pupil in every state-funded school in England — 625,648 of them in 2024/25 — so regional figures are a census, not a sample. There is no sampling error to argue about. The trade-off is that Attainment 8 is a GCSE measure, not an internationally benchmarked one, so it can compare regions with each other but not England with Estonia.

Attainment 8 scores a pupil's best eight GCSE results out of 90, with English and maths double-weighted. 2020 and 2021 are not comparable with the rest of the series — those grades were centre-assessed and teacher-assessed during the pandemic, and they inflate every region at once. They are drawn on the charts below because deleting inconvenient years is worse than labelling them, but no comparison on this page rests on them.

The result that breaks the poverty story

If economic circumstances set the ceiling, the region with the most poor pupils could not be the region with the best results for poor pupils. In England it is, by a wide margin, and has been for the whole published series.

The correlation, with and without London

The relationship between disadvantage and results across the nine regions is worth two charts rather than one, because a single number hides what is going on.

Is it a London effect or an Inner London effect?

Both, and the split is informative. Outer London has the higher overall score (50.8 against 49.9), because it has a less disadvantaged intake. Inner London has the higher score for disadvantaged pupils (43.8 against 40.3) despite 46% of its cohort being disadvantaged against Outer London's 28%.

What this adds to the Wales question

The money page eliminated economic explanations for the England–Wales gap by testing across four nations. This page tests the same idea a second way, inside one jurisdiction where policy is held constant and economics varies enormously — from £26,678 per head in the North East to £64,091 in London.

The two tests agree. Disadvantage matters a great deal to a region's average, which is why the eight-region correlation is −0.94. But it does not set a ceiling, which is why London can carry the heaviest disadvantage in the country and get the best results out of it. Somewhere between those two facts is the space that school systems occupy — and it is large enough to hold the whole England–Wales gap comfortably.

A caution on the obvious next step. It is tempting to read London's performance as proof that some particular reform works, and there is a large literature that does exactly that, mostly disagreeing with itself. The most careful work attributes much of the London effect to primary schools improving in the 1990s and 2000s, and to the capital's pupil population changing, rather than to the secondary-school interventions it is usually credited to. This page makes the weaker claim it can support: outcomes for poor pupils vary by ten Attainment 8 points across English regions running the same national policy — twelve once London is split into inner and outer — so being a poor pupil cannot be what determines them.
