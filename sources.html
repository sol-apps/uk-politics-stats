Sources and methods

Every figure on this site was read out of a published data file, not out of coverage of one. This page says which file, and what is wrong with it.

PISA

2025 scores come from the appendix data tables published alongside the national reports on 8 September 2026 — tables B1.1, B1.3 (science), C1.1, C1.3 (reading) and D1.1, D1.3 (maths), which carry England, Northern Ireland, Scotland, Wales, the United Kingdom and the OECD average as separate rows.

  • PISA 2025: national report for England — DfE, with analysis by the Oxford University Centre for Educational Assessment. Scores shown here to the nearest point; the underlying tables carry decimals.
  • PISA 2025: national report for Northern Ireland — Department of Education NI.
  • PISA 2025: national report for Scotland — Scottish Government.

2012, 2015, 2018 and 2022 scores for all four nations come from the four-nation comparison in the PISA 2022 national report for Wales, figures 9.5 to 9.7.

2006, 2009 and 2012 scores come from the appendix tables B21, C6 and D6 of the PISA 2012 national report for England, which list the UK nations individually. The 2012 column is present in two independent sources and agrees exactly in all twelve nation-and-subject cells, which is the cross-check this series rests on.

What is wrong with PISA

  • The UK exclusion rate is above the OECD limit and rising. 7.6% of sampled schools were excluded in 2025 against a 5% target, up from 4.9% in 2022 and 5.4% in 2018. The OECD accepted the 2025 results for trend comparison.
  • 2022 failed one or more sampling standards across much of the UK, and it is the comparison point for England's 13-point science rise. The official reports flag it.
  • England's sample has been found to skew high. Jerrim (2021) estimates England's PISA 2018 maths mean was about 11 points above a properly representative sample.
  • The OECD average is not one consistent series. Which countries it covers changes between cycles, which is why the trend charts on this site plot the four nations and a fixed 500 reference rather than an OECD line. Where an OECD figure is quoted it is the one published for that cycle.
  • Scores are estimates with standard errors of roughly 2 to 4 points. Differences of a few points are not differences. The comparisons this site draws conclusions from are tens of points wide.

Attainment 8 and disadvantage

Department for Education, Key stage 4 performance, academic year 2024/25, drawn through the Explore Education Statistics API from the “All state-funded pupil characteristics and geography” dataset. Regional figures are a census of every pupil in every state-funded school, so there is no sampling error. Cohort and disadvantage counts are from the same dataset's pupil totals.

  • 2020 and 2021 grades were centre- and teacher-assessed and are inflated relative to every exam year. They are drawn on the trend chart and shaded; no comparison rests on them.
  • “Disadvantaged” is the DfE definition: eligible for free school meals at any point in the previous six years, looked-after, or adopted from care. It is not the same thing as being in poverty, and it is a wider group than current free school meal eligibility.
  • Inner and Outer London are excluded from every correlation on this site, because including them alongside London would weight the capital three times.
  • Attainment 8 is a domestic measure. It compares English regions with each other and cannot be compared to a PISA score or to another nation.

Economy

Office for National Statistics, Regional gross domestic product: all ITL regions, edition 1998 to 2023, released 17 April 2025. This site uses Table 11 — chained volume measures per head in 2022 money, so the series is real rather than nominal and can be compared across years.

  • GDP per head is measured where output is produced, not where people live. Commuting inflates London's figure and depresses its neighbours'. It is a measure of regional economic activity, not of household income, and the pages here say so where it matters.
  • 2023 is the latest published year, so the 2025 PISA cycle is paired with 2023 GDP.

Poverty

Welsh Government, Relative income poverty: April 2023 to March 2025, which publishes all four UK nations on one consistent basis from the DWP's Households Below Average Income series. Children in relative low income after housing costs, three-year average to the financial year ending 2025: England 29%, Wales 32%, Northern Ireland 21%, Scotland 19%.

  • Sub-UK HBAI estimates are Official Statistics in Development. They come from a household survey, they are volatile enough that only three-year averages are published, and different publications produce slightly different figures for the same nation. A single consistent source is used here rather than the best-looking number from each.
  • After housing costs is the measure used throughout, because before-housing-costs figures understate poverty badly wherever rents are high.
  • Household income inequality is not reliably published below UK level, so the inequality question is tested with PISA's own socio-economic gradient instead.

How the statistics were calculated

Correlations are Pearson's r, computed on the figures published in the sources above. Sample sizes are given every time one is quoted, because with four nations or nine regions the sample size is the caveat. Fitted lines on scatter charts are ordinary least squares and are drawn dashed and faint, because they are an interpretation rather than an observation.

Where a correlation changes substantially when one point is removed, both numbers are given. That happens twice on this site and it is the most interesting thing in the data both times.

What this site does not claim

It does not claim that any particular education policy caused any particular result. Seven three-yearly observations of four jurisdictions cannot separate a curriculum change from an accountability change from a change in who is being taught, and no amount of chart-making fixes that. The argument runs the other way — it eliminates economic explanations rather than identifying a policy one, and stops there.
